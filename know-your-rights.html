The Short Version

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) says employers with 15 or more employees cannot discriminate against you because you're Deaf — and they must provide reasonable accommodations like interpreters, captions, or visual alerts.

Using a hearing aid or cochlear implant doesn't change your rights. The law protects you regardless.

What Employers Must Provide

These are considered reasonable accommodations under the ADA. Your employer pays for all of them:

  • ASL interpreters — in person or video remote (VRI) for interviews, meetings, training
  • Live captioning (CART) — real-time text of what's being said
  • Video Relay Service (VRS) access
  • Captions on Zoom, Teams, and all video calls
  • Visual/strobe fire alarms and emergency alerts
  • Captioned phones and hearing-aid-compatible headsets
  • Written notes for brief communications
Your employer cannot lower your pay to cover accommodation costs. That's illegal.

During the Hiring Process

  • Employers cannot ask about your disability before offering you the job
  • They can ask if you can perform the essential job functions
  • You don't have to disclose you're Deaf unless you need an accommodation for the interview
  • Employers must provide (and pay for) an interpreter for your interview
  • They cannot cancel your interview because they assume accommodations would be too expensive

Real Cases, Real Consequences

These are actual cases where employers broke the law:

Werner Enterprises (2023) — $36 million jury verdict
Refused to hire a qualified Deaf truck driver. The jury deliberated under two hours.
Amazon (2022) — $1.2 million settlement
Denied interpreters during employee training.
FedEx (2016) — $500,000 settlement
Denied an interpreter to a Deaf job applicant.
Walmart — $300,000 settlement
Denied interpreters at employee meetings.
Toys "R" Us (2013) — $35,000 settlement
Made a Deaf applicant provide her own interpreter for the interview.

The "Too Expensive" Excuse

Employers can only refuse an accommodation if it causes "undue hardship" — meaning significant difficulty or expense relative to their resources.

In practice, this almost never works for Deaf accommodations. Most are free or cheap: captions, VRI, written notes, strobe alarms. The bar is extremely high, and the employer has to prove it.

How to File a Complaint

If an employer discriminated against you:

  1. Go to eeoc.gov/filing-charge-discrimination
  2. File within 180 days (or 300 days if your state has its own civil rights agency)
  3. It's free — no lawyer needed to file
  4. The EEOC investigates and may offer mediation

Request an Accommodation (Template)

Copy, fill in, and send to your supervisor or HR:

"Because of my hearing disability, I need [specific accommodation, e.g., an ASL interpreter for team meetings] to perform my job effectively. I'm requesting this as a reasonable accommodation under the Americans with Disabilities Act."

Tip: Always make the request in writing (email counts). Keep copies of everything.
